Most of the AI attention in design tools goes to making something from nothing. Canva's new model goes after the part that comes next, when you already have a layout and only want one thing about it to change. Canva Brush, announced October 29, comes from the same in-house lab as Canva Craft, the image model we covered last week. Canva's own shorthand is that Craft generates the image and Brush refines the design around it.

## What changed

Brush is a vision-language model, meaning it looks at your design as well as reading your prompt. Canva says it understands how a design is put together and can change anything from a single line of copy to every slide in a deck.

Under the hood it works in a loop. It writes and runs code to adjust design elements, inspects what happened on the page, and uses that to decide its next step. The result stays a normal Canva design, so you can take over in the editor at any point.

To try it, open a design or attach or link one in Canva AI, then pick Brush from the dropdown. Canva suggests three places to start:

- **Restyling.** Try a different color scheme or describe a different mood for an existing design.
- **Changes across pages.** Update headers throughout a multi-page design from one prompt, without going page by page.
- **Reformatting.** Turn a presentation slide into an Instagram graphic.

On training, Canva says Brush started from a leading open-weights model, which it doesn't name. It was then post-trained on real design and editing tasks and refined with reinforcement learning, using a scoring system that judges visual quality, how well the layout follows design principles, and whether the task got done. Canva says its own professional designers set the definition of what counts as good.

## Why it matters for designers

Anyone who's asked an AI to fix one thing and watched it quietly change three others knows why this matters. Canva says the same thing in its announcement: the model has to know what needs to stay exactly as it is. If Brush really holds the rest of the design steady, that's more useful day to day than another way to generate a first draft.

The boring jobs are the obvious test. Updating every header in a deck, or adapting one approved design for a new format, is the kind of work that eats an afternoon without calling on much design judgment. Canva says Brush did particularly well on restyling and format changes in its internal testing, so those are fair places to start.

## What to watch out for

The headline numbers need their context. Canva says Brush is up to 69% faster for general design editing tasks and 46% lower cost per edit task compared to its previous design agent. "Up to" is doing some work there, the comparison is against Canva's own earlier setup, and the announcement doesn't publish a test set. It says Brush matched or beat that setup on "most" editing tasks, which also means not all of them. Run your own file through it before you trust it on a deadline.

This is a first version and it has real gaps. Canva says it's still working on Brand Kit support, referencing attachments, and handling image-heavy layouts. If your work leans on a Brand Kit or is mostly photography, you may be testing it right where it's weakest, so check fonts and colors by hand.

Pick Brush from the dropdown on purpose. Canva says it powers "supported" design edits and tells you to select it to make sure it's running your full editing experience. The announcement doesn't list which edits are supported, so don't assume that every Canva AI edit you make is coming from Brush.

One ranking in the announcement is easy to misread. Canva calls itself the world's second most advanced AI lab for image generation as measured by DesignArena, a crowdsourced comparison site. That's a claim about Craft and image generation, and it says nothing about how good Brush is at editing.
