This is a synthesis of how to run a mood board in Adobe Firefly Boards, drawn from several of Adobe's own tutorials and a few independent write-ups, plus general knowledge of the tool. It isn't any one person's process, and we haven't run it end to end ourselves. The common thread across the sources is that Boards works best when you treat it as a working board, not a gallery of generations.

## The workflow

1. **Put the brief on the board first.** Before any images, add the client brief, keywords, or shot list as text at the top. Everyone who opens the board later, including you two weeks from now, sees what it's for. Text labels and simple colored shapes are enough to group things.

2. **Pull in references from wherever they live.** You can upload files, drag them in, or search Adobe Stock from inside Boards. Photographers can go straight from Lightroom: select photos, then choose Generate in Firefly and Start a mood board from the Photo menu (the File menu in Lightroom Classic). Adobe's tutorial says that path takes up to ten images at a time.

3. **Give each artboard one job.** This is the step most boards skip. Split references into separate artboards for style, subject, and color instead of one big collage. Adobe's tutorials show selecting a group of images and collecting them onto an artboard. A focused artboard is much easier to use as a reference in the next step, because it tells the model one thing.

4. **Generate from your references, not just a prompt.** In the Generate image panel, use Sample from canvas to point at your sketch and your style or color artboard, then write a prompt that says how each one should be used, like "layout from the sketch, palette from the color board." You can pick Adobe's models or a partner model from the same panel, though partner models need a plan with premium features. If you don't know how to describe an image you like, Boards can caption it with a prompt for you to edit.

5. **Use Remix for materials, and a style reference for new directions.** Select a few images and hit Remix to blend them. Adobe's Remix tutorial shows it holding on to the base image's composition while pulling in materials and surfaces from the others, so start with the image that has the layout you want. When you want something that rethinks the angle or arrangement, use a result as a style reference with a prompt instead. The sources disagree on the details here: an older write-up describes Remix returning a fixed batch of 16, while Adobe's newer tutorial lets you pick how many and charges credits for each. It's clearly changed, so check the panel before you run a big batch.

6. **Try presets as shortcuts, not answers.** Boards has a long list of one-click presets. One independent roundup picks out a handful as useful for early ideas, with Add to Scene at the top of its list for putting a product in context, plus Product Photo, Restyle for testing looks on an existing image, and Remove Text for cleaning up a reference so you can reuse it. Expect to iterate. The same writer needed four tries to get a usable Mashup.

7. **Keep a "picked" artboard separate from the exploring.** This isn't in any of the tutorials, but it follows from how they work. A board fills up with variations fast. Move the few directions you'd stand behind onto their own artboard, so the client reviews three choices, not forty.

8. **Share for comments, and use comments instead of text boxes.** Share by link, or invite people by email with edit or comment access. Collaborators need an Adobe account, but a free one works. One write-up recommends pinned comments over loose text boxes, since people miss the boxes.

9. **Hand off to Photoshop to finish.** Once a direction is chosen, send it to Photoshop with Open copy in Photoshop, or download it and open it on the desktop. That's where layout, color, retouching, and texture happen. The board's job is to set direction, not to produce the final.

## Why it matters for designers

Most mood boards are static: you collect references, present them, and then start over in another app. The useful thing about Boards is that the references can feed generation directly. That only works if the board is organized, though. A focused color artboard steers a generation. A messy collage mostly confuses it.

It also keeps the client conversation in one place. Comments sit next to the images they're about, and the picked directions are right there when you move to production.

## What to watch out for

Don't treat the board as your archive. An Adobe Community thread from February describes boards and history disappearing for a while, with replies saying it happens occasionally and things usually come back. Keep your original references and any picks you're committing to saved somewhere else.

Adobe Stock images you pull in as references aren't licensed. If one ends up driving the final, buy it or replace it.

Most of the sources here are Adobe's own tutorials or friendly write-ups, and none of them dig into output quality or failure cases. Partner models also change often, with their own terms and costs. The sidebar has what we could and couldn't confirm about data and rights.

Credits add up quickly once you're running Remix batches and partner models. Check your balance before a session with a client watching.
