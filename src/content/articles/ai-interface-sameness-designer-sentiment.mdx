This is a synthesis of public discussion, not a tested or maker-announced fact, and not a survey of all designers. Search for what designers are actually writing about AI-generated interfaces lately, and one diagnosis keeps showing up independently, months apart, across different publications: the tools aren't broken, they're converging.

## What designers are saying

A recurring argument is that today's AI design and coding tools, whichever one you use, tend to produce the same visual grammar: rounded cards, soft gradients, a chat assistant tucked in the corner, one font used for everything, three feature cards in a row. The explanation offered independently more than once is the same. These tools are trained on overlapping datasets and optimized toward similar ideas of what "good" or "modern" design looks like, so when nothing constrains them, their output gravitates toward the statistical average of what they've already seen. The point isn't that the results are broken or ugly. It's that competence has become common, and competence alone doesn't create distinction anymore.

One thread running through this discussion is that the problem is getting worse, not better, as more tools train on each other's increasingly AI-generated output. A layout that read as fresh a year ago reads as a cliché now, not because taste changed, but because the pattern got absorbed and repeated back. A related argument holds that this shifts where design value actually sits. Generation itself has gotten cheap and fast, so what's scarce now is judgment, the ability to decide what a product should refuse to be, not just what it can produce.

The fixes people describe converge on the same idea, too: lock down real constraints, a documented design system, fixed tokens for color, type, and spacing, an explicit point of view, before letting a generative tool anywhere near production work, rather than trying to prompt your way to something distinctive. More than one piece frames this as design's job changing shape: less about producing screens, more about deciding what shouldn't exist even when a model confidently offers it.

## Why it matters for designers

If you're already leaning on AI for interface or prototype generation, this is worth treating as a warning label, not a reason to stop. The tools compress production, they don't automatically produce a point of view. Where this discussion agrees is that the fix isn't a cleverer prompt, it's giving the tool a real constraint to work inside, a design system, a locked palette, a documented set of rules, before asking it to generate anything.

## What to watch out for

This isn't a scientific survey of designers broadly. It's a pattern showing up independently across a small number of well-read design publications, not a poll of the profession. There's also a reasonable counterargument worth holding alongside it: used well, these same tools can genuinely expand how many directions a team explores before committing to one. The concern here is about unconstrained output, not generative tools themselves, and opinion on how big a problem this actually is remains split.
