Delivering a vertical cut of a horizontal edit used to mean resizing and keyframing every shot by hand. Auto Reframe in Adobe Premiere Pro analyzes each clip, works out where the action is, and builds a new sequence at the aspect ratio you pick. It's genuinely faster, and it also tends to hand back shots that drift, captions that fall off the edge, and the odd frame that follows the wrong person.

This workflow is synthesized from Adobe's own tutorial and several independent guides, plus general Premiere Pro knowledge, not from any one person's process. We haven't tested it hands-on end to end.

1. **Lock the horizontal edit first.** Auto Reframe makes a separate, duplicate sequence, so changes to your original after the fact won't carry over. Finish the cut, then reframe. If you're still shooting, frame a little wider than you normally would, with room around your subject, so there's something to crop into.

2. **Decide what happens to your text and graphics.** The guides that cover graphics agree that titles, captions, and logos are where Auto Reframe needs the most help. The approaches differ. One option is to leave graphics on their own tracks and reposition them by hand afterward, which gives you the most control. Another, suggested by a vendor guide, is to build captions as Essential Graphics pinned to a point in the frame and nest everything before reframing, so overlays stay together with their footage. Pinning saves time on long, caption-heavy edits, but it's more setup, and you'll still check every one.

3. **Run Auto Reframe Sequence.** Right-click the sequence in the Project panel and choose Auto Reframe Sequence (it's also under the Sequence menu). Pick a target aspect ratio, such as Vertical 9:16 or Square 1:1. Then choose a motion tracking setting: Slower Motion for locked-off or gently moving shots, Faster Motion for quick, busy action, or Default in between. The dialog also asks whether to nest your clips. Tutorials suggest nesting when your timeline already has a lot of motion adjustments you don't want Premiere to fight with. Click Create, and the new sequence lands in an Auto Reframed Sequences folder.

4. **Review every shot, not just the first few.** Play the new sequence through and note the shots where the crop drifts, cuts off a face, or follows the wrong subject. Shots with more than one person or fast movement are the usual suspects.

5. **Fix the drift.** Select a problem clip and open Effect Controls. The regular Motion controls are greyed out on a reframed clip, and Auto Reframe's own controls take their place. Choose the option to override the generated path, which turns the analysis into keyframes you can edit. For a shot that should feel locked-off, the simplest fix one guide recommends is deleting every position and reframe keyframe except the first, so the crop stops wandering. Then use Reframe Offset and Reframe Scale to place and size the shot. Adobe's own tutorial instead says to adjust Position, Scale, and Rotation in Effect Controls and keyframe them, and doesn't mention the override. If the Motion controls are greyed out on your clip, as Envato's guide describes, use the override and the Reframe controls instead.

6. **Do a dedicated text and graphics pass.** Go back through with only overlays in mind. Keep captions and calls to action inside the platform's safe area, away from the bottom edge where app interfaces sit, and make sure no caption lands over a face. If something shifted after a pinned setup, check its pin before moving it by hand.

7. **Borrow the tracking for pan and scan.** This is a less obvious use. If you've got 4K footage in a 1080 sequence, you may want slow pan and scan movement that follows the action. Auto Reframe won't do anything here on its own, since both are 16:9. One documented workaround is to copy the clips into a temporary square sequence, working in batches of about ten, apply Auto Reframe there, and override the generated path to bake the movement into keyframes. Copy the clips back into the 16:9 sequence and adjust Reframe Offset and Reframe Scale until the frame is filled. The movement keyframes come along with them.

8. **Export each version separately.** Make one export per platform rather than reusing a single vertical master everywhere, since safe areas and preferred lengths differ. Work from your highest-resolution source when you reframe, because a 9:16 crop from 1080p footage is already throwing away most of the frame.

## Why it matters for designers

If you make motion work for brands, the vertical version is often the one more people see. Auto Reframe takes the most repetitive part of that job off your plate, and steps 5 and 6 are where your eye still matters. The pan and scan trick in step 7 is also a reminder that a feature built for one job can do quiet work elsewhere in an edit.

## What to watch out for

Auto Reframe is a first pass, not a finished deliverable. Every guide expects some cleanup afterward, and the generated movement can add motion you never wanted, so budget time for review.

The option names have shifted between Premiere versions, and the guides use slightly different wording for the same control, so expect small differences from what's described here. Some of the sources are a couple of years old, and one is a vendor blog that ends by pitching its own tool, so weigh it accordingly. Finally, cropping a wide shot to vertical always loses most of the frame. If a shot only works wide, no amount of keyframing will save it, and it may be better to swap in a different take.
