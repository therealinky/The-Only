Substance 3D Sampler can take a single photo of a surface, like a stone wall, a piece of leather, or a weathered plank, and turn it into a full PBR material: base color, normal, height, roughness, and more. The AI-powered version of that feature does a lot of the heavy lifting. What it doesn't do on its own is give you something that repeats without seams, sits at the right scale, and looks right under new light.

This guide pulls together Adobe's own documentation and tutorials, Poly Haven's published standards for photo-based textures, and independent write-ups on making materials from photos and from AI-generated images, plus general Sampler know-how. It's a synthesis, not a process we've tested end to end ourselves, and we point out where the sources take different approaches.

1. **Shoot the surface for a material, not a nice photo.** The goal is a flat, evenly lit image with as little light and shadow baked in as possible. Shoot square-on to the surface so there's no perspective to undo, and on an overcast day or in soft, even light rather than direct sun. Adobe's tutorial says a smartphone works as well as a DSLR for this. Two extra habits help later: put something with a known size in a test shot so you can set the real-world scale, and include a gray card or other neutral surface so you can check white balance. If you're out and about, Adobe Capture on your phone can preview the result and send it to Sampler.

2. **Pick the right algorithm.** Drag the photo into Sampler and choose Image to Material. It has two modes. **AI Powered** uses machine learning to recognize shapes and generate normal, height, and roughness maps, and it also strips shadows and highlights out of the base color. Since Sampler 4.2 you can tell it what kind of material you're feeding it, like fabric, wood, or leather, which helps it generate more accurate channels, so try a couple of categories and keep the best. **B2M** is the older procedural method. Adobe says it can be less accurate but works on a wider range of images. If AI Powered gets confused by an unusual surface, B2M is your fallback.

3. **Work at a lower resolution, then crop.** Adobe notes that the AI mode takes longer on high-resolution images and suggests using Sampler's layer resolution settings to keep things responsive while you build the stack. Next, add a Crop filter to trim any stretched or distorted edges before you deal with tiling. Sampler filters stack like Photoshop layers: each one affects everything under it.

4. **Make it tile.** Add **Make it Tile**. It overlays offset copies of your material and blends the seams with patches you control. Threshold, Smoothness, Contrast, and Color Equalizer do most of the work, and Height Matching changes how the height maps blend (check it in the 2D view's height channel). Then turn up texture tiling in the viewer, to around four repeats, to see what really happens at scale. Adobe's docs say Make it Tile works best on even, grid-like surfaces with no single eye-catching spot. If yours has one, try the separate Tiling filter instead, which works differently.

5. **Hunt down the repeat.** A material can tile without seams and still give itself away with one distinctive crack or knot showing up over and over. Use **Equalize** to even out uneven lighting or color across the photo, then **Clone Stamp** over anything that stands out too much. It's the step that turns a quick material into a usable one.

6. **Delight only if you used B2M.** Adobe's **Delight (AI Powered)** filter removes lighting from the base color and has no settings. The docs are specific about order: put it at the top of the stack, after extracting the channels and making the material tile. They also say not to use it with AI Powered Image to Material, which already includes a delighting pass.

7. **Check it on a real shape, then expose what you'll tweak.** Look at the material on a sphere and a flat plane under a few lighting setups, not just the default. Then pin the parameters you'll want to adjust later, like a target color, roughness, or normal intensity, so they show up as simple controls when you use the material elsewhere.

8. **Export the right way for where it's going.** Export as an SBSAR if you want to keep those exposed controls in other Substance apps, or as bitmaps for Blender, a game engine, or a web 3D tool. If you need more resolution, Sampler's AI Upscale can double or quadruple it, and Adobe recommends running it on data at its original resolution rather than something you've already resized.

**No photo of the right surface?** Sampler's Generative (Beta) panel has Text-to-Texture and Image-to-Texture. Image-to-Texture takes a reference image of any aspect ratio and gives back square, tiling textures, four variations at a time, and you can run Image to Material on the result. People were already doing a version of this with earlier image generators. It's a useful starting point, but a generated texture isn't a measured surface, so treat the scale and color as guesses.

## Why it matters for designers

Stock material libraries are great until a client needs their actual brick, their packaging board, or their upholstery fabric. A photo plus Sampler gets you there quickly, and the extra steps above are what separate a material that looks fine on a thumbnail from one that holds up on a full-screen product render.

The steps also transfer. Once you understand delighting, tiling, and checking for repeats, you'll spot the same problems in materials from any tool, including the AI texture generators that keep showing up inside 3D apps.

## What to watch out for

The sources set very different quality bars, and that's worth knowing. Adobe's tutorials show a phone photo and a few filters producing a usable material in minutes. Poly Haven's published standards, aimed at materials meant to look right in any scene, ask for much more: color calibrated against a chart, real-world dimensions measured accurately, very high resolution reached without upscaling, and ideally a cross-polarized flash rig to remove reflections. Neither is wrong. Decide which one your project needs before you start.

The AI mode has quirks. Adobe's own troubleshooting page says AI Powered results sometimes come out with the wrong colors, and suggests toggling GPU-accelerated neural networks in Sampler's preferences. Delighting can't fully rescue a photo shot in hard sunlight, so a better source photo usually beats more filters. And some of the independent write-ups we drew on are a few years old, so menus and filter names may have moved since. Adobe has been moving its Sampler docs to Experience League, which is the place to check current names.
