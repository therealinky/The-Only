Figma's agent has been working in Figma Design for a while. As of November 23 it's also generally available in FigJam and Figma Slides, the two places a lot of design work actually gets talked about and sold. This is based on Figma's release note, blog post, and help pages. We haven't tried it ourselves.

## What changed

In FigJam, Figma's examples include setting up a brainstorming board, building a journey map, grouping sticky notes into themes, and putting action items into a table with owners and due dates. The headline trick is turning a messy board into an organized Spec with a summary and next steps. If you've used FigJam AI's older sort and summarize tools, this goes further: it can build and restructure the board, not just tidy what's on it. Those older tools are still free, while the agent's own board summary costs about 50+ credits.

In Figma Slides, you can start a deck from an attached Spec outline and a brand template, review the outline, then generate the deck. Once it exists, you can ask the agent to split crowded slides, sharpen takeaways, shorten copy, draft speaker notes, adapt the deck for a different audience, give feedback on the story, or estimate how long the talk will run.

Both can pull in outside context. You add it through "Add context," either by attaching images and files or by picking a connector. Figma's connector page lists more than 20 verified partners, including Slack, Asana, Hex, Notion, Linear, Google Drive, and Dovetail, and you can add your own on a paid plan. Connector tools that write back to those outside services are off by default.

## Why it matters for designers

The unglamorous part of design work, turning a workshop into something people act on and turning that into a deck someone approves, takes real hours. A board-to-Spec-to-deck path inside one tool is the kind of thing that saves an afternoon after every workshop, if the output is good enough to edit rather than redo.

Connectors are the bigger shift. A journey map built from your actual research notes in Dovetail, or a status deck seeded from the real tasks in Asana, starts closer to the truth than one built from a blank prompt. That also means it's only as good as what's in those tools.

For designers who'd rather not hand any of this to an agent, nothing changes. FigJam and Slides still work as they did, and the agent only does something when you prompt it.

## What to watch out for

Credits add up faster here. Figma's own examples put a single FigJam diagram at about 180+ credits and a 10-slide deck at about 250+, and restyling a deck costs about the same. A Professional full seat gets 3,000 a month, but a Collab seat, which is what a lot of people in FigJam and Slides have, gets only 500, enough for about two generated decks. Either way, a few rounds of "make it again, but shorter" can eat a real share of that. Figma's credits page also says undoing an AI action doesn't refund the credits.

The help pages are honest about gaps. In FigJam, the agent can't create or edit widgets, embeds, or templates, can't run timers or voting, and can't send a board over to Figma Design, Slides, or Make for you. In Slides, templates and reference decks are only partly supported, so generated slides may not match your template exactly, and audio and video attachments aren't supported. That matters if you're expecting on-brand slides straight out of the box. Charts are a similar story: the agent can build one from a spreadsheet screenshot, but it draws it with editable shapes rather than a native or data-linked chart, so check the values and proportions. Figma's FigJam help page also says the agent only works in the desktop app or a browser, not on mobile.

Be careful with what you connect. Figma's connector page explicitly warns about prompt injection when pulling context from outside sources, and a Slack channel or shared doc can contain anything. Keep write tools off unless you need them, and think twice before pointing the agent at a client's channels. The sidebar covers what we couldn't confirm about data handling and plans.
