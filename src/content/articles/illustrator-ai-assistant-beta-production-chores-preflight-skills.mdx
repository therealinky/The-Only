Illustrator 31.0, announced on November 10, adds an AI Assistant in beta. You describe a production job in plain language and it does the clicking, from tidying layers to setting up a print file. We haven't tried it ourselves, so this is based on Adobe's own announcement posts and the replies under them.

## What changed

You open the assistant from the Application Bar, then either describe what you want or start from a preset like Recolor, Organize, Export, Setup, or Fit Text. Adobe's examples cover the jobs that usually eat the end of a project: organizing layers and artboards, recoloring artwork, exporting assets, fixing spelling, setting up a print document, and vectorizing an image.

It can also read files you attach. Adobe lists Illustrator files, Markdown, spreadsheets (XLSX or CSV), and pasted images or screenshots. Its examples include pulling swatches or styles out of another Illustrator file and turning each row of a spreadsheet into its own artboard, which is the kind of variable-data job that used to mean scripting or a lot of duplicating.

There's a preflight check too. It looks at fonts, text, images and links, color, print and bleed, layout, and accessibility, then sorts what it finds into critical issues, warnings, and passed checks. If you ask, it can fix many of the critical ones.

If you find yourself asking for the same thing every week, you can save that prompt or workflow as a Skill and call it up later by typing a slash in the prompt field. Longer jobs run in the background while you keep working elsewhere in the file.

The same release adds new Blend panel options with easing handles on the canvas, a refreshed set of brushes, styles, patterns, and gradients, and an in-app Quick Skill Guide with short lessons on shape, color, and type.

## Why it matters for designers

Most of what's here isn't about making artwork. It's about the tedious stuff around it: renaming fifty layers before handoff, exporting a set of icons at every size a developer asked for, catching the missing link or RGB image before the file goes to the printer. That's where a lot of studio time actually goes, and it's work that's easy to describe in a sentence but slow to do by hand.

Skills are the part worth watching. A saved, repeatable prompt is close to a lightweight action or script that anyone on a team can run, without anyone having to write code. If they hold up, they could make a studio's house export or print-prep routine much easier to share.

For anyone skeptical of generative AI, it's worth noticing that most of these tasks don't generate anything. You can use the organizing, exporting, and preflight parts without letting it draw a single shape.

## What to watch out for

It's a beta, and Adobe's post says not to edit the same objects the assistant is working on while a longer job runs. Save a copy before pointing it at a client file, and check the result before you trust it, especially anything it says it fixed in preflight.

Generation is capped. Adobe says the assistant currently supports up to 15 artwork generations per day, and it points to an FAQ for credits and limits that we couldn't open to confirm. Check how many credits your plan includes before building it into a routine.

The replies so far are few and mixed. One person called it and the new blend handles real time-savers for production work. Others asked what the AI was trained on, or why Adobe is leaning on AI at all when its strength is human creativity. Adobe's community manager answered that the tools are meant to cut manual steps, not replace creative direction. None of the replies under Adobe's two posts mention bugs yet, but it's early.
